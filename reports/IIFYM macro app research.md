# Build an IIFYM coach that teaches itself away

IIFYM rests on solid evidence: with calories and protein matched, body composition comes out about the same whether the diet is low-fat or ketogenic ([ISSN 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5470183/)). Setting macros is therefore mostly arithmetic. Protein comes first at **~1.6–2.2 g/kg** for both sexes. Fat gets a floor of **~20–30% of kcal or ≥0.5 g/kg**, and carbs fill whatever is left. The hard part is estimating maintenance and reading the scale correctly. The Macro Experiment gets this right by having users "baseline" maintenance from their own intake and weigh-ins instead of trusting a calculator ([Step 3](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-3-baselining-and-training.html)). Its 2014-era claim that reverse dieting "raises metabolism" is out of date. The only RCT found reverse dieting did no better at limiting regain than going straight to maintenance ([JISSN 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12381988/)). An app built on these principles would automate baselining as an adaptive TDEE estimate, run phase logic (cut 0.5–1%/wk, "loading"/bulk 0.25–0.5%/wk, maintenance, optional reverse) and explain each weekly adjustment in plain language. It would also include what large apps get wrong: free barcode scanning, a verified database (with South African foods), forgiving progress metrics and an eating-disorder-safe mode. Adaptive-coach competitors charge **$72–100/yr**. MyFitnessPal charges South Africans near US parity at about **R1,400/yr** ([AppTail ZA](https://apptail.io/app/myfitnesspal-calorie-counter-Zr/south-africa)), which leaves room for a local product at **R119–149/mo or R799–999/yr**. **Caveat on sources:** almost all figures come from search-engine snippets, because full-page fetches were blocked in the research sessions. Prices, the textbook equations and several thresholds are flagged as unverified below and should be checked before you ship.

## Protein first, fat floor, carbs fill: setting macros for men and women

The Macro Experiment is an older blog, mostly from around 2014. It sells no app, coaching or programme, and it sends readers to Layne Norton's coach list and to Avatar Nutrition ([Resources](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/resources.html)). Its method has three stages. First, users learn to log properly: weigh food in grams, check MyFitnessPal entries against package labels (the author estimates **~10% of MFP entries are wrong**) and ignore MFP's own calorie goal ([Step 2](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-2-learning-how-to-count-macrosiifym.html)). Second, users find maintenance empirically. They weigh in every morning and track everything for 7 days. If weight ends the week where it started, the average daily intake is their TDEE, because "there is no online IIFYM calculator that can accurately do this" ([Step 3](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-3-baselining-and-training.html)). Third, they set macros: protein **1–1.1 g/lb** of bodyweight, fat **20–30%** of kcal, carbs as the remainder and fibre at roughly 20–25% of bodyweight in lb (as grams, capped near 60 g) ([Step 4](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-4-calculating-your-individual-macros.html)).

The baselining idea holds up better than any single equation. It is simply a manual version of what MacroFactor and Avatar now automate. Two parts need updating, though. Seven days is too short given daily water swings, so 2–3 weeks of trend data is more robust. And 1–1.1 g/lb of total bodyweight (≈2.2–2.4 g/kg) is at or above the top of the evidence range. In the Morton 2018 meta-analysis of 49 RCTs, lean-mass gains plateaued at **~1.62 g/kg/day** (95% CI ~1.03–2.20), with 2.2 g/kg suggested as a "maximise" target ([Morton 2018](http://breathe-edu-downloads.s3.amazonaws.com/Morton-2018.pdf)). The ISSN recommends **1.4–2.0 g/kg** for exercising adults ([Jäger 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5477153/)). Lean lifters in a deficit are the exception: they need **2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass**, scaling up with leanness and deficit size ([Helms 2014 review](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-systematic-review-of-dietary-protein-during-in-a-Helms-Zinn/711decd066be689a40a1f5b5a4af3dce40c736af)). For users with obesity, base protein on goal weight or fat-free mass, or targets become inflated.

When there is no baseline yet, use a predictive equation as a starting estimate. **Mifflin-St Jeor** is the most reliable classic equation, but it is no oracle. In the Frankenfield review it put only about 56% of people within ±10% of measured RMR, barely ahead of Harris-Benedict ([PubMed 15883556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15883556/)). In a 2020 study of women the figure was 71% ([PMC7299486](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7299486/)). For lean, trained users with a credible body-fat figure, **Cunningham** (500 + 22 × FFM kg) had the smallest error in athletes. Even so, every equation underestimated their RMR ([athlete RMR study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318280022_The_accuracy_of_resting_metabolic_rate_prediction_equations_in_athletes)). The activity multiplier adds the most error, and the error runs in both directions. Measured against doubly labelled water, prediction equations underestimate TEE in active people ([Sci Rep 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-66767-7)). Lay users, meanwhile, overrate their own activity. TDEE also tops out at about **2.0–2.4 × REE** even in very active people ([Westerterp](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/exercise-energy-expenditure-and-energy-balance-as-measured-with-doubly-labelled-water/A00FAD78FB18AC376B94DAE63B5024BB)). The Mifflin coefficients (men: 10·kg + 6.25·cm − 5·age + 5; women: same − 161) and the standard multipliers (1.2 / 1.375 / 1.55 / 1.725 / 1.9) were **written from memory, not verified** this session.

**Men and women differ in absolute numbers, not in per-kg rules.** Protein per kg is sex-neutral. Women simply end up with lower gram targets because they weigh less and carry less lean mass. For women, the hormonal risk that matters is **low energy availability**, not fat percentage as such. Trials that cut fat to about 20% of kcal saw only small hormonal shifts ([PMC2063009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2063009/)). By contrast, energy availability below **~30 kcal/kg FFM/day** is linked to suppressed LH and menstrual disturbance ([PMC4342163](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4342163/)). A practical fat floor is max(20% kcal, 0.5 g/kg), with women kept at or above ~0.6–0.8 g/kg on long cuts. That is a coaching convention, not a trial threshold. The menstrual cycle moves RMR only slightly: about +30–120 kcal/day in the luteal phase, inconsistently ([Frontiers 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1778735/full); [Benton 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7357764/)). Phase-specific macros are therefore not justified. Weekly averages and like-for-like cycle comparisons of weight are. Older adults need at least **1.0–1.2 g/kg** of protein ([ESPEN/PROT-AGE](https://www.clinicalnutritionespen.com/article/S2405-4577(16)30203-0/fulltext)). For perimenopausal lifters, 1.2–1.6 g/kg is a reasonable extrapolation.

Worked starting points follow. Assumptions: age 30, man 85 kg/180 cm, woman 65 kg/165 cm, desk job, lifting 3–4×/week, multiplier 1.55. Calculations are the researcher's own, using Mifflin, 4/4/9 kcal/g and the evidence ranges above.

| Person | Maintenance | Cut | Lean bulk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Man, 85 kg (BMR ≈ 1,830) | 2,840 kcal · 153P / 79F / 380C | 2,340 · 187P / 65F / 252C (≈0.55% BW/wk) | 3,090 · 153P / 85F / 428C |
| Woman, 65 kg (BMR ≈ 1,370) | 2,125 · 117P / 71F / 254C | 1,725 · 143P / 52F / 171C | 2,325 · 117P / 65F / 318C |

A conservative 1.4 multiplier would put maintenance at about 2,560 and 1,920 kcal instead. Treat the real number as lying somewhere in that band until 2–3 weeks of trend data narrow it down.

## Phases: cut slowly, load slower, and treat reverse dieting as optional

**Cutting.** Aim for **0.5–1.0% of bodyweight per week**, slower when leaner, to keep muscle ([Helms, Aragon & Fitschen 2014](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20/tables/1)). Use "the smallest possible deficit that yields appreciable weight loss," because metabolic adaptation scales with the deficit ([Trexler 2014](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1550-2783-11-7)). True adaptive thermogenesis, measured once weight is stable, is small: **−54 ± 105 kcal/d** in Martins 2020 ([PMC7458773](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7458773/)). It feels larger mid-diet because non-resting expenditure (largely NEAT) can drop about 30% while a reduced weight is held ([Rosenbaum & Leibel review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13679-016-0237-4); review-table figure, verify). Diet breaks have the best evidence of any cutting add-on. In MATADOR, men with obesity alternating 2 weeks of restriction with 2 weeks at maintenance lost **12.3 vs 8.0 kg fat** compared with continuous restriction ([PMC5803575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575)). In lean trained athletes (ICECAP), 1-week breaks mainly cut hunger and irritability and improved alertness ([PMC7906362](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362)). Expect a fast week-1 drop that is mostly water and glycogen, then a slower, noisy trend. Reasonable signals to pause a cut are falling performance, poor sleep, low mood or libido, any menstrual disruption, or a rate below 0.25%/wk after two downward adjustments. These are coach heuristics without primary sources.

**Bulking ("loading").** Target **0.25–0.5% BW/week** on a **10–20% surplus** for novice and intermediate lifters, and less for advanced ones, for whom ~2 kg/month "may be excessive" ([Iraki 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/)). Helms 2023 randomised trained lifters to maintenance, a 5% surplus or a 15% surplus for 8 weeks. Muscle thickness did not differ between groups, but the 15% group gained more skinfold fat. Across participants, the rate of weight gain predicted fat gain strongly and muscle gain only weakly ([Helms 2023](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/de93156f-23a9-492a-8a85-438233a7e6bf/full)). The message for users: **faster loading mostly adds fat**. Expect a **1–2 kg jump in the first 1–2 weeks** from glycogen, water and gut content. That is not fat, and the algorithm must not respond by cutting calories. Realistic lean-gain ceilings fall from about 0.75–1 kg/month for beginners to 0.1–0.3 kg/month for advanced lifters. Those figures come from non-peer-reviewed aggregators ([Fitbod](https://fitbod.me/blog/muscle-gain-in-a-month/)). Ending a bulk at about 15–20% body fat (men) or 25–30% (women) and following with a 2–6-week mini-cut is a coach heuristic with no primary source.

**Maintenance and recomposition.** Maintenance is a phase in its own right. Helms 2023 found no muscle-thickness penalty at maintenance versus a surplus over 8 weeks. That supports a "recomp at maintenance" mode for beginners, detrained lifters and people with higher body fat. In this mode the scale barely moves, so waist measurements, photos and strength become the progress signals.

**Reverse dieting.** The Macro Experiment recommends adding about 2–3% of calories at a time, mainly from carbs, holding protein steady and pausing whenever weight climbs, with the aim of raising metabolism without fat gain ([Step 5](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-5-reverse-dieting.html)). The current evidence does not support the metabolic claim. The 2025 JISSN preliminary RCT found the reverse-diet group regained relatively *more* than the comparison groups (not significant), and the authors saw no clear advantage over returning straight to maintenance ([PMC12381988](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12381988/); sample size and increments unverified). Stronger By Science's review, "Reverse Dieting: Hype Versus Evidence," calls it a slower walk to maintenance, not a metabolic fix ([via MetaFilter](https://metafilter.com/196554/Reverse-Dieting)). The Conversation reaches the same conclusion ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/reverse-dieting-slowly-increasing-calories-wont-prevent-weight-regain-but-may-have-other-benefits-144316)). The app should therefore default to **"return to maintenance"** at the adaptive TDEE. It can offer **"gradual reverse"** as an appetite and comfort tool: about 10% per week per Healthline, or the Macro Experiment's 2–3% steps, capped at TDEE + 10% ([Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/reverse-dieting)). It should never be framed as "repairing" metabolism. MacroFactor has only a help article on how to run a reverse diet manually, which suggests it has no native mode ([MacroFactor FAQ](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/collections/9-frequently-asked-questions)). That leaves a small, honest niche for a well-explained reverse mode.

## Explaining IIFYM: a budget, a GPS and a four-rung ladder

The clearest explanation is financial. **Calories are the monthly budget. Protein is rent, paid first and every day. Carbs and fat are groceries and fun money, and you choose the mix.** "If it fits" means any purchase is allowed while the budget balances. Spend the whole budget on takeaways, though, and you are short on essentials like micronutrients and fibre. That leads straight into the main criticism of IIFYM: in one small study, more flexible dieters than strict dieters missed several RDAs ([ISSN 2017 abstract](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5596410/)). It also explains why the 80/20 framing is needed: about 80% minimally processed foods and about 20% discretionary. The second metaphor covers estimation. The calculator gives a starting route, and the **weekly trend weight is the GPS that recalculates it**.

Onboarding should climb a ladder rather than present six numbers on day one. Week 1 is honest logging with no targets, which also works as the Macro Experiment baseline. Weeks 2–3 add calories (±100) and protein (±10 g). Fat and carb ranges come next, then fibre and food quality. This is practitioner consensus; no trial compares it with full macros from day one. Early expectation-setting matters. In Noom's company-affiliated studies, reading lessons was associated with fewer unrealistic weight-loss expectations ([JMIR mHealth 2021](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2021/11/e30622/PDF)). The beginner mistakes worth flagging are: treating the calculator TDEE as truth, choosing "very active" for three gym sessions a week, not logging oils, sauces and drinks, weekend overeating that erases the weekday deficit, mixing up cooked and raw weights, and cutting faster than 1% a week ([Ideal Nutrition](https://www.idealnutrition.com.au/flexible-dieting/); [macronutrientcalculator.org](https://www.macronutrientcalculator.org/blog/flexible-dieting-iifym/)).

## App plan: an adaptive coach that teaches, then steps back

### MVP and phased roadmap

The MVP is the smallest product that delivers the IIFYM loop well. It has six parts:

1. **Onboarding:** goal, stats, training age, optional body-fat %, and an ED-risk screen.
2. **Fast logging:** a verified-first database seeded with SAFOODS plus retailer barcodes, **free barcode scanning**, copy-yesterday and saved meals, and offline caching.
3. **Daily weigh-in** with a trend line.
4. **Targets** using the protein-first/fat-floor/carb-remainder logic, shown as remaining numbers.
5. **A weekly adaptive check-in** with a plain-language report.
6. **About 20 just-in-time lesson cards** tied to events.

Phase 2 (months 4–6) adds phase planning (cut → diet break → maintenance/reverse → load → mini-cut), Apple Health and Health Connect sync, the "fill my macros" suggester, recipe URL import, weekly budget distribution, and a knowledge-level tracker with short quizzes. Phase 3 (months 7–12) adds AI photo logging as a confirm-before-save draft with an oil and sauce prompt, a grounded AI Q&A coach, a coach dashboard with client sharing for the hybrid tier, and outreach to Discovery Vitality. Defer cycle-aware targets. The evidence supports cycle-aware *expectations*, not different macros, and POPIA's draft health and sex-life regulations add compliance cost ([CDH Oct 2025](https://www.cliffedekkerhofmeyr.com/en/news/publications/2025/Sectors/Technology-Communications/technology-and-sourcing-alert-7-october-Draft-Regulations-Relating-to-the-Processing-of-Data-Subjects-Health-or-Sex-Life-by-Certain-Responsible-Parties-in-terms-of-Section-1122c-of-the-Protection-of-Personal-Information-Act-Published-on-26-September)).

### Core algorithms

The algorithm follows MacroFactor's published principle: **TDEE ≈ mean logged intake − (Δ trend weight × energy density)**, computed from trend weight, not raw scale weight, and **adherence-neutral**, so missed targets never block an adjustment ([MacroFactor help #20](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/20-expenditure); [#222](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)). MacroFactor says it needs 3–4 weeks of data before it is confident that expenditure has changed. The flat 7,700 kcal/kg rule overstates the energy content of early and lean-person weight change ([Hall & Chow](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3810417/)), so ρ varies by phase. Every constant below is a **design inference**, not a published value. MacroFactor does not publish its own.

```csharp
// Prior (week 0): Mifflin (or Cunningham if lean + credible BF%) × conservative PAL (1.4–1.55 for desk + 3–5 lifts)
trend[t]   = trend[t-1] + 0.1 * (clampOutlier(w[t], trend[t-1], 0.03) - trend[t-1]);  // EWMA α≈0.1, interpolate gaps
slope      = OLS(trend, last 14–28 days)                       // kg/day
rho        = phaseWeek <= 3 || lean ? 5500 : (gaining ? 6000 : 7700);   // kcal/kg, inferred from Hall
measured   = mean(intake, window, excludeFlaggedIncompleteDays) - slope * rho;
tdee       = clamp(0.8 * tdeePrev + 0.2 * measured, tdeePrev - 150, tdeePrev + 150);
target     = tdee + goalRateKgPerWk * rho / 7;                 // goalRate = %BW preset × trendWeight
if (abs(target - currentTarget) < 50 || phaseWeek <= 2) target = currentTarget;  // settle water/glycogen
target     = applyGuardrails(target, phase, user);
macros     = protein(g/kg by phase, FFM/goal-weight anchor if obese) → fat = max(0.2*kcal/9, 0.5*kg, womenLongCut ? 0.7*kg : 0) → carbs = remainder
```

The phase logic is a small state machine.

| Phase | Rate preset | Protein | Exit/transition triggers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cut | 0.5% (lean: men <15% BF, women <23%), 0.75% standard, 1.0% only if higher BF | 2.0–2.4 g/kg (or 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM if lean) | Goal reached; 8–16 wks elapsed; rate <0.25%/wk after 2 cuts; red flags → offer diet break (1–2 wks) or maintenance |
| Maintenance / recomp | ±0.1–0.2%/wk drift tolerated | 1.6–2.2 g/kg | User-chosen; minimum 2–4 wks after a cut |
| Reverse (optional) | +2–10%/wk, carbs first | Held constant | Reaches adaptive TDEE (cap TDEE + 10%) |
| Load (bulk) | Beginner 0.5%, intermediate 0.25–0.35%, advanced ≤0.25%; surplus ≤ +20% TDEE | 1.6–2.2 g/kg | BF heuristic ceiling; rate overshoot 2 wks running → reduce surplus; offer mini-cut |

The guardrails are hard-coded, not left to configuration:

- A calorie floor of the higher of **1,200 kcal (women) / 1,500 kcal (men)** and **BMR × 1.1**. This is common practice, unsourced.
- A loss rate capped at 1% BW/wk.
- No deficit targets for under-18s, pregnancy or breastfeeding, with a referral instead.
- A menstrual-disruption flag that forces a move to maintenance.
- The LLM never sets or changes targets. Only the deterministic engine does.

### Education, coaching and progress design

Education follows the MacroFactor/Carbon pattern of **teaching at the moment of need**. Every check-in report states what changed, why, which data drove it and what to do next ([MacroFactor check-ins](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/247-introduction-to-check-ins-and-coaching-modules); [Carbon check-in](https://help.joincarbon.com/en/articles/6004812-weekly-check-in)). On top of that sit event-triggered cards: a scale spike after a high-carb day triggers a 60-second explanation of glycogen and water. Phase transitions ship with their own "what to expect" scripts. "Knowledge" is modelled as a concept graph (energy balance → protein priority → trend weight → adaptation → phase logic → reverse dieting myths → 80/20). Each concept is marked *seen → applied → mastered* through micro-quizzes and observed behaviour, such as logging oils after the oils card. Mastery unlocks fewer prompts and the macro-ladder rungs, which builds in a "graduation" path similar to MacroFactor's Fast Check-In. No app was found that publicly documents a knowledge tracker or progressive macro unlocking. It is a differentiator, but unvalidated. Evidence for spaced repetition in consumer nutrition is thin ([T&F 2021](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/suppl/10.1080/19325037.2021.1930613), title only), so keep quizzes light.

For coaching, the evidence favours a hybrid model. In an observational study of about 65,000 HealthifyMe users, AI plus a human coach produced about **2.12 kg more loss over 3 months** than AI alone, mostly by making tracking more consistent ([INFORMS 2026](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/Research-Finds-that-AI-Weight-Loss-Apps-Work-Best-When-Combined-with-Human-Coaching)). In a 7-week RCT, LLM chat features alone showed "little to no effect" ([arXiv 2511.20652](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.20652v1)). The AI coach should therefore be retrieval-grounded in the app's vetted lesson corpus and the user's own data, should refuse or escalate on ED cues, and should avoid unprompted calorie talk.

For progress, put the **trend line first, with raw weigh-ins as faded dots**. Show waist, photos and strength as non-scale signals. Use forgiving consistency metrics ("logged 5 of 7 days") instead of fragile streaks. Self-monitoring frequency is the most reliable correlate of weight loss ([Burke 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21185970/)), while broken long streaks may trigger abandonment (unverified secondary source, [Yu-kai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/streak-design-gamification-motivation-burnout/)). Logging effort and portion uncertainty are the main reasons people quit food logging ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8s81j1rk/qt8s81j1rk.pdf?t=rwqa29)). Logging speed is therefore a retention feature, not a convenience.

Keep it to one deployable API plus Functions. Do not split it into microservices.

## Risks that can sink the product

**Eating-disorder harm** is the largest ethical and reputational risk. Mitigations: an onboarding screen (SCOFF-style) that routes flagged users to a non-numeric mode, hard floors and rate caps, no red "over" states, the option to hide weight, and LLM guardrails. A 2026 preprint found up to **30%** of LLM answers to neutral diet prompts were unsafe, rising to **68%** in risky contexts, and that calorie mentions increased "food noise" ([arXiv 2606.02444](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02444); not peer-reviewed).

**POPIA** treats health data as *special personal information* (s26). For a wellness app, the realistic legal basis is specific, informed, unbundled consent that can be withdrawn in-app (s27), obtained before collection. Bundled consent is the most common defect in SA health apps ([Webber Wentzel](https://webberwentzel.com/News/Pages/popia-compliance-for-health-data-navigating-special-personal-information-requirements-in-healthcare.aspx); [MJK Inc](https://mjkinc.co.za/software-technology-law/healthtech-contracts)). Separate consents are needed for weight/food, cycle data and AI processing. Sending data to an offshore LLM is a cross-border transfer that needs safeguards. Get a local privacy lawyer to review before launch.

**AI accuracy** cuts both ways. Photo estimates undercount by about a third, and the adaptive engine has no independent validation, MacroFactor's included ([calorie-trackers.com](https://calorie-trackers.com/reviews/macrofactor/)). Systematic under-logging biases the TDEE estimate low even as the target self-corrects ([MacroFactor #140](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/140-do-i-need-to-log-everything-i-eat-and-drink-to-have-an-accurate-expenditure-and-use-macrofactor-s-coaching-features)). Present every number as an estimate with a range.

**Unverified inputs** must be confirmed before shipping: the Mifflin and Harris-Benedict coefficients and PAL table, fibre targets, calorie floors, all competitor and ZAR prices (check the ZA storefronts by hand), the CalZA and FitFoodz database claims, and the full text of the 2025 reverse-diet RCT.

## Conclusion

The defensible product is not "a better MyFitnessPal" but **an automated version of The Macro Experiment's baselining method, with the 2014 metabolism myths removed and the reasoning shown to the user every week**. MacroFactor already owns the adaptive-TDEE idea internationally. What nobody combines is that engine, an explicit curriculum that measures what the user has learned, honest phase coaching including a reverse mode that doesn't overpromise, and an ED-safe design. In South Africa, that combination plus a verified local database, offline-first logging and PPP pricing faces no direct adaptive-coach competitor in the sources found.

The strategic risk is retention, not acquisition. Health & Fitness converts trials well, but most users are gone by day 30 and most annual subscribers don't renew. The "teach then step back" design therefore has to *earn* the renewal. The app should let users graduate to lighter tracking rather than lose them to burnout, and should make a year-end phase plan the reason to stay.
