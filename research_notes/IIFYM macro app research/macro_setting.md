## 1. What The Macro Experiment teaches

### Takeaway
The Macro Experiment (themacroexperiment.com) is an older personal blog (most material is from about 2014) built around a 5-step DIY IIFYM guide. It **rejects online calculators** in favour of "baselining" maintenance calories from a week of tracked intake plus daily weigh-ins. It then sets protein at 1–1.1 g/lb, fat at 20–30% of calories and carbs as the remainder, and promotes slow "reverse dieting" (adding about 2–3% calories at a time). I found no current app, paid programme or pricing on the site. It refers readers to outside coaches (Layne Norton's list, Avatar Nutrition and others) and does not appear to sell its own coaching.

### Cited Findings
- **Structure:** a step-by-step "IIFYM Guide" ([IIFYM Guide](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/iifym-guide.html)). The steps are: Step 2 Learning to count macros ([Step 2](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-2-learning-how-to-count-macrosiifym.html)), Step 3 Baselining and training ([Step 3](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-3-baselining-and-training.html)), Step 4 Calculating your individual macros ([Step 4](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-4-calculating-your-individual-macros.html)) and Step 5 Reverse dieting ([Step 5](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-5-reverse-dieting.html)). It also has a "Metab Rehab" section ([Overview](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/metab-rehab-an-overview.html), [Important Terms](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/metab-rehab-important-terms.html)).
- **Anti-calculator stance:** the site states "There is no online IIFYM calculator that can accurately do this." Instead it says to find maintenance empirically ([Step 3](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-3-baselining-and-training.html)).
- **Baselining method:**
  - Weigh yourself each morning on waking.
  - Track all food in MyFitnessPal for 7 days.
  - Add up the daily calories and divide by 7 to get average intake.
  - The aim is to end the week at the same weight as day 1, which makes that average your maintenance (TDEE) ([Step 3](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-3-baselining-and-training.html)).
- **Tracking advice:**
  - Weigh food in grams.
  - Check MyFitnessPal entries against package labels, because the author estimates MFP entries are wrong about 10% of the time.
  - Ignore MFP's own calorie goal at the top of the diary.
  - Track for a few days before baselining.

  ([Step 2](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-2-learning-how-to-count-macrosiifym.html))
- **Macro formula (Step 4)** ([Step 4](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-4-calculating-your-individual-macros.html)):
  - Protein: 1 to 1.1 g per lb of current bodyweight.
  - Fat: 20–30% of total calories.
  - Carbs: all remaining calories.
  - Fibre: roughly 20–25% of bodyweight (in lb, as grams), capped around 60 g.
- **Worked example on the site:** a 110 lb person maintaining at 1,260 kcal gets 121 g protein (1.1 × 110), 35 g fat (25% ÷ 9) and 115 g carbs. A second baselining week, hitting these macros exactly, then confirms that weight holds steady ([Step 4](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-4-calculating-your-individual-macros.html)).
- **Reverse dieting:**
  - Add calories slowly, mainly from carbs plus some fat.
  - Keep protein steady at about 1 g/lb.
  - Start with additions of 2–3% of calories, conservatively, and pause when weight climbs.
  - The stated goal is to raise metabolism without gaining fat.

  ([Step 5](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-5-reverse-dieting.html); [Reverse Diet category](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/blog/category/reverse-diet))
- **Coaching:** the author coached herself. She recommends Dr. Layne Norton or coaches on his list, Alpha Training Protocols, Katie Ringley and BestFitBody. She documented her own reverse diet with Avatar Nutrition, an algorithm that adjusts macros weekly from weigh-ins ([Resources](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/resources.html); [Avatar post](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/blog/im-published-and-reverse-dieting-with-avatar-nutrition)).
- **Other content:**
  - An interview with Layne Norton ([Interview](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/the-macro-experiment-interviews-dr-layne-norton)).
  - An "IIFYM Chain Restaurant Master List" ([link](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/blog/the-iifym-chain-restaurant-master-list)).
  - Vacation tracking tips ([Week 8](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/blog/week-8-tips-for-tracking-macros-on-a-vacation)).
  - Competition-prep logs ([Prep wk 7.5](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/blog/competition-prep-week-75)).

### Inferences
- The baselining approach is in effect a manual version of what adaptive apps such as MacroFactor and Avatar now automate: estimating TDEE from intake and weight trend. Its spirit holds up better than any single equation, but 7 days is short given day-to-day water swings. 2–3 weeks of trend data would be more robust.
- 1–1.1 g/lb of **total** bodyweight (2.2–2.4 g/kg) sits at or above the top of the evidence range for maintenance or bulking (about 1.6–2.2 g/kg; see section 3). It fits a cutting phase, but is generous and possibly hard to sustain for people with higher body fat. For those users, goal weight or lean mass is a better anchor.
- The "reverse dieting to raise metabolism" framing reflects 2014-era thinking. Current evidence-based sources treat reverse dieting mainly as a psychological and behavioural tool. See the gaps below.

### Gaps
- I found no evidence that the site currently sells coaching, an app or programmes, and found no pricing. The site appears dormant since about 2014–2016, but I could not fetch it to confirm.
- The author's name appeared in one search summary as "Cait Roberts". This is not verified.
- I found no controlled-trial evidence this session that reverse dieting "raises metabolism" beyond the normal recovery of NEAT and body mass.

## 2. BMR/RMR formulas, accuracy, and activity multipliers

### Takeaway
For the general population, **Mifflin-St Jeor** is the default. It is the most often accurate of the classic equations, but only about 55–80% of people land within ±10% of measured RMR. For lean, trained people with a known body-fat percentage, **Cunningham** (or Katch-McArdle) does better, though all equations tend to underestimate RMR in athletes. Activity multipliers add the largest error. Treat any calculated TDEE as a starting estimate and correct it within 2–4 weeks from the real weight trend.

### Cited Findings
- **Formulas**
  - **Mifflin-St Jeor (1990)** (from memory, not verified this session):
    - Men: 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age + 5
    - Women: 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age − 161
    - Original paper discussed at [mifflinstjeor.com](https://mifflinstjeor.com/mifflin-st-jeor-equation/) and [healthycalculatorhub](https://healthycalculatorhub.com/guides/mifflin-st-jeor).
  - **Harris-Benedict, revised by Roza & Shizgal 1984** (from memory, not verified this session):
    - Men: 88.362 + 13.397 × kg + 4.799 × cm − 5.677 × age
    - Women: 447.593 + 9.247 × kg + 3.098 × cm − 4.330 × age
  - **Katch-McArdle:** 370 + 21.6 × LBM(kg) ([ResearchGate review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/400838949_The_Katch-McArdle_Formula_for_TDEE_Estimation_Applications_in_Sports_Science_and_Bodybuilding_Research)).
  - **Cunningham:** 500 + 22 × FFM(kg) ([Recreational-athlete validation](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266569237_Resting_Energy_Expenditure_Prediction_in_Recreational_Athletes_of_18-35_Years_Confirmation_of_Cunningham_Equation_and_an_Improved_Weight-Based_Alternative); [Nutrium](https://nutrium.com/blog/cunningham-equation-for-nutrition-professionals/)).
- **Frankenfield et al. 2005 systematic review (non-obese and obese adults)**
  - Mifflin-St Jeor was the most reliable equation. It predicted RMR within 10% of measured RMR in more people than any other equation and had the narrowest error range.
  - The authors warn of "noteworthy errors" when it is applied to individuals and to some age and ethnic groups.
  - Within-10% rates in one comparison: Harris-Benedict 57.5%, Mifflin 56.4%, WHO/FAO/UNU 55.2%, so the advantage is modest.
  - Source: ([PubMed 15883556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15883556/))
- **2020 study of 125 women:** Mifflin-St Jeor was the most accurate equation at the individual level. It predicted RMR within 10% of measured RMR in 71% of women, and in 80% of women with obesity ([J Nutr Sci / PMC7299486](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7299486/)).
- **Accuracy by sex, BMI, age and ethnicity:** a separate analysis of four RMR equations exists ([ScienceDirect, J Sci Med Sport 2011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1440244011000417)). Its details were not retrieved.
- **Athletes**
  - In collegiate athletes, all equations significantly underestimated RMR. Cunningham had the smallest mean error (−165 kcal) ([ResearchGate: accuracy of RMR equations in athletes](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318280022_The_accuracy_of_resting_metabolic_rate_prediction_equations_in_athletes)).
  - In recreational athletes aged 18–35, Cunningham was confirmed with r = 0.86 ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266569237_Resting_Energy_Expenditure_Prediction_in_Recreational_Athletes_of_18-35_Years_Confirmation_of_Cunningham_Equation_and_an_Improved_Weight-Based_Alternative)).
  - Common equations were built in non-athletic groups and may underestimate needs in bodybuilders ([ClinicalTrials.gov NCT07878052](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07878052)).
- **Activity multipliers and TDEE**
  - A 2024 Scientific Reports validation tested predictive TEE equations against doubly labelled water. Equations generally underestimated TEE, and the error grew with higher TEE and higher physical activity level. Error also depended on body composition ([Nature Sci Rep 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-66767-7)).
  - A secondary summary of the same validation says only about 43% of individuals landed within ±10% ([kcalm.app, secondary](https://kcalm.app/blog/tdee-activity-multipliers-overestimate/)).
  - From DLW data, TDEE tops out at about 2.0–2.4 × REE even in people with strenuous work or high leisure activity. Activity energy expenditure is the most variable part of TDEE ([Westerterp, Proc Nutr Soc](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/exercise-energy-expenditure-and-energy-balance-as-measured-with-doubly-labelled-water/A00FAD78FB18AC376B94DAE63B5024BB)).
  - Practitioner guidance: if a deficit gives less weight loss than expected over several weeks of accurate tracking, the activity level was probably overestimated ([Fitness Volt activity guide](https://fitnessvolt.com/tdee-calculator/activity-level-guide/)).
  - Conflict on direction: lay calculators are often said to overestimate TDEE because users overrate their activity. The DLW validation instead found that equations underestimated TEE in measured active people. Both can be true. The error is two-sided and individual: self-rated activity is the problem, not the maths.
- **Adaptive approach:** MacroFactor ties targets to the logged weight trend and adjusts them for weight-gain and weight-loss goals ([MacroFactor help](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)).

### Inferences
- Recommended app logic:
  1. Use Mifflin-St Jeor by default. Switch to Cunningham or Katch-McArdle only if the user enters a credible body-fat percentage and is lean or trained.
  2. Pick a conservative multiplier. Steer most desk workers who lift 3–5×/week toward about 1.4–1.55, not 1.725+.
  3. Recalibrate TDEE from the weight trend after 2–3 weeks. A 7-day moving average of weight against average intake is the practical method.
- Standard multipliers, from memory and not verified this session: sedentary 1.2, light 1.375, moderate 1.55, very 1.725, extra 1.9.

### Gaps
- No direct head-to-head between Katch-McArdle and Cunningham was found.
- The exact Mifflin and Harris-Benedict coefficients and the multiplier table were not verified from a primary source this session (stated from memory above).
- MacroFactor's own published accuracy data for its expenditure algorithm was not retrieved.

## 3. Sex differences: protein, fat minimums, carbs, cycle, menopause, fibre

### Takeaway
Protein needs per kg are **essentially the same for men and women**:
- About 1.6 g/kg (95% CI about 1.0–2.2) maximises lean-mass gains in surplus or maintenance.
- 2.2 g/kg is a safe upper target.
- 2.3–3.1 g/kg of **fat-free mass** applies for lean people in a deficit.

Fat should usually stay at about 20–30% of kcal (or about 0.5–1.5 g/kg). The stronger evidence for women's hormonal health concerns **total energy availability**, not fat percentage specifically. Carbs fill the remainder. The menstrual cycle makes only a small difference to energy needs (luteal RMR is about 30–120 kcal/day higher, inconsistently). Older or post-menopausal adults need at least 1.0–1.2 g/kg protein.

### Cited Findings
- **Morton et al. 2018 meta-analysis** (49 RCTs, 1,863 participants, at least 6 weeks of resistance training, no intentional energy restriction)
  - Protein above about **1.62 g/kg/day** gave no further gains in fat-free mass. The 95% CI was about **1.03–2.20 g/kg**, and the authors suggest about 2.2 g/kg for those wanting to maximise gains.
  - Supplementation was more effective in young or trained people than in older or untrained people.
  - Sources: ([Morton 2018 PDF](http://breathe-edu-downloads.s3.amazonaws.com/Morton-2018.pdf); [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/protein-science/))
- **Stronger By Science's protein update** argues for moving beyond a rigid "1.6–2.2 g/kg" rule, considering context such as energy status and body composition ([SBS](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/protein-science/)). Its full argument was not retrieved.
- **ISSN Position Stand: Protein and Exercise (Jäger et al. 2017)** recommends at least about **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** for most exercising people ([PMC5477153](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5477153/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8)).
- **ISSN Position Stand: Diets and Body Composition (Aragon et al. 2017)** ([PMC5470183](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5470183/)):
  - Diets from low-fat to ketogenic can be similarly effective when calories and protein are matched.
  - Fat loss is driven by a sustained caloric deficit. The higher the baseline body fat, the more aggressive the deficit can be.
  - Protein well above standard athletic recommendations may further improve body composition.
- **Helms, Zinn, Rowlands & Brown 2014 (IJSNEM) systematic review:** resistance-trained lean athletes in a caloric deficit need **2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass**. Needs scale upward with greater leanness and a larger deficit ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-systematic-review-of-dietary-protein-during-in-a-Helms-Zinn/711decd066be689a40a1f5b5a4af3dce40c736af); [Menno Henselmans review](https://mennohenselmans.com/eric-helms-protein/)). An updated systematic review with meta-regression on protein in energy-restricted trained people also exists ([NSCA SCJ](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/9900/effect_of_dietary_protein_on_fat_free_mass_in.179.aspx)). Its findings were not retrieved.
- **Helms, Aragon & Fitschen 2014 (JISSN), natural bodybuilding contest prep** ([PMC4033492](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4033492/)):
  - Set calories so bodyweight drops about **0.5–1% per week** to maximise muscle retention.
  - Creatine, caffeine and beta-alanine are useful supplements.
  - The paper's widely quoted macro figures (protein 2.3–3.1 g/kg LBM, fat 15–30% kcal) were **not confirmed** in search snippets. One secondary page gave different figures. Verify these against the full text.
- **Off-season and physique-athlete recommendations**
  - Iraki, Fitschen, Espinar & Helms 2019 (Sports 7:154): protein **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day**, about 0.40–0.55 g/kg per meal across 3–6 meals, including within 1–2 hours before and after training ([PDF](https://elementssystem.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/iraki-nutrition-bodybuilders-2019-sports-07-00154.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334044740_Nutrition_Recommendations_for_Bodybuilders_in_the_Off-Season_A_Narrative_Review)).
  - "Nutritional Recommendations for Physique Athletes" (2020): surplus giving **0.1–0.25% bodyweight gain per week**; protein 1.6–2.2 g/kg; carbs **at least 3 g/kg**; fat **0.5–1.5 g/kg/day** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339175003_Nutritional_Recommendations_for_Physique_Athletes)).
- **Fat and women's hormones**
  - Low-fat trials aiming below about 20% of kcal fat show only modest hormonal effects:
    - A 1-year isocaloric trial found no substantial change in midluteal ovarian hormones ([Gann 2003, Cancer](https://acsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cncr.11735)).
    - A 2-year trial in 220 women at about 21% fat found small changes, with some longer cycles ([PMC2063009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2063009/); [PubMed 3480374](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3480374/)).
    - A pooled estimate put the reduction in estradiol at about 7.4% (per search summary).
  - Stronger evidence concerns **energy availability**:
    - Energy availability below about **30 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day** is associated with suppressed LH pulsatility and menstrual disturbance, and raising intake has been linked to reversal ([PMC4342163](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4342163/)).
    - Increased intake restored menses in athletes with exercise-associated amenorrhea ([PMC4145292](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4145292/)).
- **Menstrual cycle**
  - Benton et al. 2020 meta-analysis (26 studies, 318 women): a small but significant rise in RMR in the luteal phase (ES 0.33). In studies since 2000 the effect was smaller and not significant (ES 0.23, p = 0.055) ([PLOS One / PMC7357764](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7357764/)).
  - A 2026 systematic review reports luteal increases of about **30–120 kcal/day**, found significant in 4 of 5 studies that saw a trend ([Frontiers in Physiology 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1778735/full); [PubMed 41971666](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41971666/)).
  - One study found luteal REE only 40 kcal higher, which was not significant ([PubMed 39086066](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39086066/)).
  - Energy intake rises more than RMR: luteal intake is about 168 kcal/day higher on average (per search summary of a meta-analysis on intake; [News-Medical, secondary](https://www.news-medical.net/health/How-the-Menstrual-Cycle-Affects-Appetite-Metabolism-and-Nutrition.aspx)).
- **Perimenopause and menopause**
  - PROT-AGE (2013) and ESPEN recommend **1.0–1.2 g/kg/day** for healthy older adults (65+), and more for active, ill or sarcopenic people ([Clin Nutr ESPEN](https://www.clinicalnutritionespen.com/article/S2405-4577(16)30203-0/fulltext); [secondary 2026 summary](https://www.clinicalnutritionreport.com/articles/protein-targets-older-adults/)).
  - No guideline sets a menopause-specific protein figure (per same summary).
  - Many older UK adults fall short of these intakes ([PMC7151458](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7151458/)).
- **Fibre:** The Macro Experiment uses about 20–25% of bodyweight (lb) in grams, capped around 60 g ([Step 4](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-4-calculating-your-individual-macros.html)).

### Inferences
- **Protein per kg is sex-neutral.** Women just get a lower absolute number because they weigh less and have less lean mass. For people with obesity, base protein on goal weight, or about 1.6–2.2 g/kg of FFM, to avoid inflated targets.
- **Fat floor in an app:** a defensible default is **max(20% kcal, 0.5 g/kg)**, i.e. about 0.25 g/lb. Do not encourage women below about 0.6–0.8 g/kg (about 0.3–0.35 g/lb) during long cuts, as a conservative buffer. This floor is a practitioner convention, not a trial-derived threshold.
- **Cycle-syncing:** cycle-specific macro changes are not justified by the evidence. At most, allow about 100–150 kcal of flexibility in the late luteal phase, or simply use weekly averages. Expect water-weight swings in that phase, so compare weight trends cycle-to-cycle.
- **Perimenopause and menopause:** prioritise resistance training and protein of at least 1.2–1.6 g/kg (the upper end extrapolated from active-adult data). Expect lower NEAT and TDEE.

### Gaps
- **Fibre targets were not verified from primary sources this session.** The common references are about 14 g per 1,000 kcal (US Dietary Guidelines) and an Adequate Intake of 38 g/day for men and 25 g/day for women under 50 (IOM). Both are from memory.
- I found no strong evidence base for a specific "0.3 g/lb fat minimum for women". It is a coaching heuristic.
- No menopause-specific protein RCT consensus was found.

## 5. Criticisms and limitations of IIFYM; the 80/20 approach

### Takeaway
IIFYM's core claim is well supported: body composition is driven by energy balance plus protein, and diets of very different macro make-up perform similarly when those are matched ([ISSN 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5470183/)). Its weaknesses are practical:
- **Micronutrient gaps.** Flexible dieters are more likely to miss RDAs.
- **The "junk food is fine" misreading.**
- **Fibre and satiety being ignored.**
- **Tracking error.**
- **Risk for people prone to disordered eating.**

The 80/20 framing addresses most of these: about 80% of calories from minimally processed, nutrient-dense foods and about 20% discretionary.

### Cited Findings
- **Macro-agnostic results:** diets from low-fat to ketogenic produce similar body composition when calories and protein are matched. The deficit is the driver ([Aragon et al. 2017 ISSN](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5470183/)).
- **Micronutrients in bodybuilders** (2017 ISSN conference abstract, 30 male bodybuilders, flexible vs strict dieters): mean nutrient intakes did not differ significantly. However, **a greater proportion of flexible dieters failed to meet RDAs for several nutrients**. The sample was small and recruited online, and this is an abstract only ([ISSN 2017 Proceedings, PMC5596410](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5596410/)).
- **IIFYM does not distinguish foods by micronutrient content** ([macronutrientcalculator.org](https://www.macronutrientcalculator.org/blog/flexible-dieting-iifym/); [Texas Real Food review](https://discover.texasrealfood.com/diet-decoder/iifym-diet)).
- **Flexible dietary restraint:** a 2019 Appetite study reportedly links flexible (as opposed to rigid) dietary restraint with lower BMI, less overeating and less psychological distress. This is cited by a blog and the primary paper was not located ([bulknutrients blog](https://www.bulknutrients.com.au/blog/weight-loss/science-iifym-how-flexible-dieting-actually-leads-to-long-term-fat-loss)).
- **Tracking risks:** sources warn that tracking can fuel anxiety or disordered eating, and that people with a history of restrictive eating should involve a clinician. User-submitted database entries are often wrong ([Ideal Nutrition](https://www.idealnutrition.com.au/flexible-dieting/); [macronutrientcalculator.org](https://www.macronutrientcalculator.org/blog/flexible-dieting-iifym/)). The Macro Experiment estimates MFP entries are wrong about 10% of the time ([Step 2](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-2-learning-how-to-count-macrosiifym.html)).

### Inferences
- **"Flexible dieting" ≠ "eat junk to fit macros".** The original intent was to permit treats within a mostly whole-food diet.
- An app can operationalise 80/20 by adding:
  - a fibre target, a whole-food or produce target, or a micronutrient score;
  - a "discretionary calories" budget of about 10–20% of kcal.
- Keep "fat" and "carb" as soft ranges, with protein and calories as the hard targets.

### Gaps
- I could not locate the primary papers this session for:
  - Stewart, Williamson & White 2002 and Westenhoefer 1999 (rigid vs flexible restraint);
  - Conlin et al. 2021 (flexible vs rigid dieting in resistance-trained people).

  Verify these before citing.

## 6. How to explain IIFYM to a beginner

### Takeaway
Present calories as a **budget**, protein as a **fixed bill you pay first**, and fat and carbs as **flexible spending**. Start beginners on a progressive ladder:
1. Log honestly with no targets.
2. Hit calories plus protein only.
3. Add fat and carb ranges.
4. Optionally add fibre and an 80/20 food-quality rule.

Correct expectations early: the scale fluctuates, and the calculator number is a first guess.

### Cited Findings
- **Budget framing:** the macro budget "lives inside" the calorie target, which comes from TDEE. Cutting means subtracting about 300–500 kcal and gaining means adding about 200–400 kcal. Treat the output as a 2-week starting experiment and adjust from measured results (search summary of generic guides; [calcplanet](https://www.calcplanet.com/health/macro-calculator/); [si.com](https://www.si.com/showcase/nutrition/how-to-count-macros)).
- **Common beginner mistakes** ([Ideal Nutrition](https://www.idealnutrition.com.au/flexible-dieting/); [macronutrientcalculator.org](https://www.macronutrientcalculator.org/blog/flexible-dieting-iifym/); [leanffmi](https://leanffmi.com/flexible-dieting-tips/)):
  - inaccurate logging and trusting user-submitted entries;
  - eyeballing portions instead of using a scale;
  - "saving" macros all day for one big meal, which leads to overeating and poor protein distribution;
  - ignoring fibre;
  - setting a discretionary budget too small for real social life;
  - obsessive tracking.
- **Weigh food in grams, check entries against labels, and baseline before setting targets** ([Macro Experiment Step 2](https://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-2-learning-how-to-count-macrosiifym.html); [Step 3](http://www.themacroexperiment.com/step-3-baselining-and-training.html)).
- **Protein distribution:** about 0.4–0.55 g/kg per meal across 3–6 meals ([Iraki 2019](https://elementssystem.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/iraki-nutrition-bodybuilders-2019-sports-07-00154.pdf)).
- **Hormonal water swings:** luteal-phase changes to intake and RMR are real but small ([Benton 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7357764/)).

### Inferences
Suggested beginner script and analogies:
- **Bank account:** calories are your monthly budget. Protein is rent, paid first and every day. Carbs and fat are groceries and fun money, so you choose the mix. "Fits your macros" means any purchase is allowed if the budget still balances, but a budget spent entirely on takeaway leaves you short on essentials (micronutrients and fibre).
- **Fuel gauge or GPS:** the calculator gives a starting route. Your weekly average weight is the GPS that recalculates it.
- **Progressive tracking ladder:**
  - Week 1: log everything you eat, with no changes.
  - Weeks 2–3: hit calories ±100 and protein ±10 g.
  - Week 4 onward: add fat and carb ranges.
  - Later: fibre at about 14 g per 1,000 kcal (unverified; see gap in section 3) and 80/20 food quality.
- **Expectation setting:**
  - Use the 7-day average weight, not daily readings.
  - Expect 1–2 kg of water and glycogen change when carbs or sodium shift.
  - Women should compare weight at the same cycle phase.
- **Bigger beginner mistakes to flag:**
  - Treating the calculator TDEE as truth.
  - Picking "very active" because of 3 gym sessions.
  - Not logging oils, sauces and drinks.
  - Weekend overeating erasing the weekday deficit.
  - Weighing cooked vs raw inconsistently.
  - Cutting too aggressively (more than 1% bodyweight per week).
  - Women dropping fat very low, or energy availability too low, on long cuts.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found comparing "progressive" vs "full macros from day one" onboarding for adherence. The ladder approach is practitioner consensus, not trial evidence.
- MacroFactor, Renaissance Periodization and Examine.com beginner guides were not retrievable this session. Only MacroFactor's help page on goal adjustments surfaced.
