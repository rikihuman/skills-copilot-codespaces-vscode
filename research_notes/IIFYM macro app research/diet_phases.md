## Reverse dieting: definition, mechanism, evidence, criticisms, protocols

### Takeaway
Reverse dieting means slowly adding calories after a cut (often ~50–150 kcal/week or ~10%/week) on the theory that this "restores" metabolism without fat regain. The only randomized trial so far (JISSN 2025, preliminary) found it did **not** limit regain better than eating ad libitum or returning straight to maintenance. The evidence-based framing, from Stronger By Science and MacroFactor, is that reverse dieting is just a slow walk to maintenance. It can be offered for psychological or appetite reasons, but the app should not claim it "fixes" metabolism. For most users, the default should be to jump to estimated maintenance or close to it.

### Cited Findings
- **Definition:** a gradual increase in caloric intake after a diet, "to help restore metabolic rate towards its previous levels" — [Stronger By Science podcast ep. 96 page](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/podcast-episode-96/); Cleveland Clinic describes it as a gradual, measured calorie increase intended to limit weight regain — [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/reverse-dieting)
- **Common protocol:** some practitioners increase intake by about 10% per week. One dietitian source says there is "no one-size-fits-all" reverse diet protocol — [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/reverse-dieting); [Today's Dietitian](https://www.todaysdietitian.com/ask-the-expert-reverse-dieting/)
- **Origin of the mechanism claim:** Trexler, Smith-Ryan & Norton (2014), "Metabolic adaptation to weight loss: implications for the athlete," JISSN 11:7. During energy restriction, changes in hormones, mitochondrial efficiency and energy expenditure act to "minimize the energy deficit, attenuate weight loss, and promote weight regain." This is the paper reverse-dieting advocates usually cite. A secondary source says it suggests gradually raising intake may let adaptations reverse without immediate fat regain (I could not confirm this wording in the paper itself) — [JISSN PDF](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1550-2783-11-7); [secondary summary](https://getfitcraft.com/science/reverse-dieting-research)
- Trexler 2014 also recommends using "the smallest possible deficit that yields appreciable weight loss," because adaptation scales with the deficit (quoted via a secondary source) — [JISSN PDF](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1550-2783-11-7)
- **First RCT (2025, preliminary):** "The effects of reverse dieting on mitigating weight regain after a caloric deficit: a preliminary analysis," JISSN 2025, DOI 10.1080/15502783.2025.2550185. Reverse dieting was compared with ad libitum intake after a deficit. The reverse-diet group regained relatively *more* weight, though the difference was not significant. The authors concluded that gradual increases were not clearly better than less structured approaches or an immediate return to estimated maintenance — [PMC12381988](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12381988/) (abstract via search; full text not fetched — verify sample size and kcal increments)
- A three-arm design (gradual reverse diet vs immediate maintenance vs ad libitum, attributed to "Da Silva et al.") appeared in search results. I could not verify its details — [Bodyspec summary](https://www.bodyspec.com/blog/post/reverse_dieting_protocols_and_evidence)
- **Registered trial:** NCT03560635 appeared in searches as a reverse-dieting trial registration — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://classic.clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT03560635)
- **Stronger By Science / Trexler critique:** the article "Reverse Dieting: Hype Versus Evidence" answers "no" to whether reverse dieting "supercharges" the metabolic rate of weight-reduced people. Summaries describe the critique as: reverse dieting "is not a metabolic fix but a slower maintenance phase," and metabolic adaptation affects weight loss but "does not make it impossible, nor is it a substantial barrier to long-term weight maintenance" — [MetaFilter quoting SBS](https://metafilter.com/196554/Reverse-Dieting); [summary](https://summify.io/discover/reverse-dieting-hype-vs-evidence-mEtmnQ/); [SBS ep. 96 "Why Reverse Dieting Doesn't Work Like You Think"](https://globalplayer.com/podcasts/episodes/7DrnVpL)
- A secondary summary reports a bodybuilder case series in which reverse dieting *delayed* recovery compared with faster post-show regain (unverified) — [search summary of Bodyspec](https://www.bodyspec.com/blog/post/reverse_dieting_protocols_and_evidence)
- Evidence is "limited and mostly anecdotal" (Healthline). Reverse dieting requires precise tracking, and muscle retention depends more on how you ate before it than on the reverse diet itself — [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/reverse-dieting); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/reverse-dieting)
- The Conversation: slowly increasing calories "won't prevent weight regain but may have other benefits" — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/reverse-dieting-slowly-increasing-calories-wont-prevent-weight-regain-but-may-have-other-benefits-144316)
- MacroFactor has a help article, "How would I pursue a reverse diet in MacroFactor" (content not retrievable this session) — [MacroFactor help #32](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/32-how-would-i-pursue-a-reverse-diet-in-macrofactor)

### Inferences
- **App design:** offer two post-cut options.
  - **Default: "Return to maintenance."** Set intake to the current adaptive TDEE estimate, optionally plus 5–10% to account for expected recovery of NEAT and glycogen.
  - **Optional: "Gradual reverse."** Add kcal each week, mostly from carbs and fats with protein held constant. Cap the add-back so it never exceeds estimated maintenance plus a small buffer. Frame the gradual option as an aid to appetite and adherence, not a metabolic repair.
- **The "50–150 kcal/week" figure:** this is a common coach protocol (Layne Norton and similar sources). I did not find a primary source giving it in this session; Healthline's "~10%/week" is the closest cited figure.
- **Expected scale jump:** after any calorie increase, expect a 1–2% scale-weight jump from glycogen, water and gut content that is not fat. Users who reverse slowly may mistake this for fat gain. The same water/glycogen logic appears in the cutting and bulking sections below.
- **The Macro Experiment:** its framing could not be retrieved (site blocked, not found in search). It is commonly associated with Jordan Pollock / "reverse dieting into a bulk" coaching, but this is unverified.

### Gaps
- Full-text details of the 2025 JISSN RCT (n, kcal increments, duration, RMR/body comp results) — PMC blocked.
- Peos et al.: I found no reverse-dieting-specific Peos paper. Peos's work is on diet breaks (ICECAP; see Cutting section).
- Menno Henselmans' specific position: not found in this session. He is generally known to argue that you should go straight to maintenance, but this is unverified here.
- What The Macro Experiment says: blocked.
- Vargas-Molina 2023: cited secondarily. Content unknown.

## Cutting: rate of loss, scale behaviour, adaptation, diet breaks/refeeds, protein, length, stop signals

### Takeaway
The standard target is **0.5–1.0% of bodyweight per week**, slower when leaner. Protein should be about **2.3–3.1 g/kg of lean mass**, and the deficit should be the smallest that still produces visible progress. True adaptive thermogenesis is real but modest when measured at weight stability: roughly −50 kcal/d (Martins 2020). It looks much larger during active dieting, mainly because non-resting expenditure (NEAT) falls about 30% in Leibel/Rosenbaum data. Diet breaks (2 weeks on / 2 weeks at maintenance, MATADOR) improved fat loss in men with obesity. In lean athletes (ICECAP), they mainly improved hunger, mood and leg endurance.

### Cited Findings
- **Rate:** Helms, Aragon & Fitschen (2014, JISSN 11:20) recommend weekly losses of **0.5–1% of body weight** to maximize muscle retention. Slower loss may better preserve lean mass in leaner athletes — [JISSN table](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20/tables/1); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/723f44d2-7c20-4214-ba62-987339099039/full)
- **Protein on a cut:** **2.3–3.1 g/kg lean body mass** (Helms 2014). A later review notes that only two underlying studies used more than 2 g/kg, so the upper end is weakly supported — [JISSN table](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20/tables/1); [JISSN 2015 review PDF](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12970-015-0111-x)
- **Deficit size:** use the smallest deficit that yields appreciable loss, because adaptation scales with the deficit — [Trexler 2014](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1550-2783-11-7)
- **Magnitude of adaptive thermogenesis (measured at weight stability):** Martins et al. 2020 (AJCN; overweight women; measured after 4 weeks of weight stability) found **−54 ± 105 kcal/d** after weight loss. In the complete-data subset it was −43 ± 119 kcal/d, and it was not significant at 1 and 2 years. It correlated with fat-mass loss but did not predict regain — [PMC7458773](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7458773/); [PubMed 32386226](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32386226/)
- A 2022 *Obesity* study from the same group found that metabolic adaptation predicted a **longer time to reach the weight-loss goal**. It matters for how long a cut takes even if it doesn't drive regain — [summary](https://summify.io/discover/entities/study/martins-and-colleagues)
- **Larger adaptation during maintenance of reduced weight (Leibel/Rosenbaum):** after maintaining 10% weight loss, 24-h energy expenditure fell about **15%** and **non-resting energy expenditure about 30%**. REE was decreased or unchanged and the thermic effect of feeding was unchanged. Non-resting expenditure is "the compartment most affected" — [Rosenbaum & Leibel review PMC4965234 / Curr Obes Rep 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13679-016-0237-4) (figures from a review table — verify)
- Declines in energy expenditure "persist well beyond the period of dynamic weight loss" (Rosenbaum 2008) — [record](https://vufind.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c508t-36ee58a5db24f608751b89c0845587674ccb8a674962a7c99a9fdb11e0acba723/Details); disputed by Hall: "no consistent evidence of a disproportionately low REE in long-term successful weight-loss maintainers" — [PMC3673773](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3673773)
- **MATADOR (Byrne et al., Int J Obes 2018):** men with obesity; 16 weeks of energy restriction delivered either continuously or as 2 weeks on / 2 weeks at energy balance. Per-protocol (n=36) weight loss was **14.1 vs 9.1 kg**, and fat loss was **12.3 vs 8.0 kg**, favouring intermittent restriction. FFM loss was similar. The REE drop adjusted for body composition was smaller with intermittent restriction — [PMC5803575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575)
- **MATADOR2 (women, presented 2025):** completers lost **10.4 vs 8.5 kg**, favouring intermittent restriction — [ESA-SRB-ANZOS 2025 abstract](https://esa-srb-anzos-2025.p.asnevents.com.au/days/2025-10-22/abstract/129073)
- **ICECAP (Peos et al.):** resistance-trained athletes. The main paper, *MSSE* 2021;53(8):1685–1698, compared continuous restriction with 1-week diet breaks every 3 weeks (main outcomes not retrieved). The secondary analysis (PLoS ONE 2021, n=26) found that after a 1-week diet break, **lower-body muscle endurance improved**, strength was unchanged, fat mass was not significantly affected, and body weight, FFM and REE rose slightly. Hunger fell (P=0.017), as did prospective consumption (P=0.020) and irritability (P=0.041), while alertness improved (P=0.003). The authors suggest using breaks later in a cut, when appetite threatens adherence — [PMC7906362](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362)

### Inferences
- **Suggested app rate presets:** a "gentle" preset of 0.5%/wk is advisable when the user is lean (e.g., <15% BF for men, <23% for women). "Standard" is 0.75%/wk. An "aggressive" preset of 1.0%/wk should be limited to users with higher body fat or a short timeline. In absolute terms, 0.5% of 80 kg is 0.4 kg/wk, a deficit of roughly 400–450 kcal/d using about 7,700 kcal/kg early. See the energy-density caveats in the TDEE section.
- **Scale behaviour to explain to users:**
  - Week 1 often shows a large drop from glycogen, water and gut content. This is not fat and should be excluded from rate judgments.
  - Daily fluctuations of ±1–2% from sodium, carbs, bowel contents and training soreness are normal.
  - Plateaus followed by "whooshes" are anecdotal water-retention effects, not established physiology.
  - In women, the luteal phase and menses commonly produce transient retention of about 0.5–2 kg. Compare like-for-like cycle weeks or use a trend.
  - These points come from background knowledge and were not confirmed with primary citations this session.
- **Cut length:** a practical guideline is 8–16 weeks per block, then 2–8+ weeks at maintenance. Diet breaks of 1–2 weeks every 4–12 weeks are reasonable based on MATADOR and ICECAP, with breaks more valuable when leaner and hungrier.
- **Refeeds** (1–2 high-carb days): far less evidence than full diet breaks. Present them as an optional adherence tool.
- **Signs to stop or pause a cut:**
  - Persistent loss of training performance or strength
  - Sleep disruption, high hunger, or low libido or mood
  - Menstrual cycle disruption in women (a red flag for low energy availability)
  - Reaching goal body fat, or the rate dropping below 0.25%/wk despite two consecutive downward adjustments
  - These are heuristic triggers; I did not find primary sources for specific thresholds this session.
- **Background knowledge, not verified this session:** Garthe et al. 2011 (IJSNEM) compared slow (~0.7% BW/wk) and fast (~1.4%/wk) loss in athletes; the slow group gained more lean mass. Rossow et al. 2013 and the natural bodybuilder case study literature document hormonal and performance declines near stage leanness.

### Gaps
- ICECAP primary outcomes (fat-loss and FFM differences between continuous and intermittent restriction): not retrieved.
- Primary citations for menstrual-cycle water retention and "whoosh" effects.
- Direct NEAT measurements (e.g., step-count decline during cuts): only the Leibel/Rosenbaum non-resting EE figure was found.

## Bulking ("loading"): rates by training age, fat:muscle ratio, water jump, mini-cuts, stop thresholds, women

### Takeaway
Target **0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week**, using a 10–20% surplus over maintenance, for novice and intermediate lifters (Iraki 2019). Advanced lifters should use the lower end or less; about 2 kg/month is likely excessive for them. Helms 2023 found that a 15% surplus produced more fat, with little extra muscle, compared with a 5% surplus or maintenance. Across individuals, faster weight gain tracked skinfold (fat) gain, not muscle thickness. Expected muscle-gain ceilings fall from roughly 0.75–1 kg/month for beginners to about 0.1–0.3 kg/month for advanced lifters.

### Cited Findings
- **Iraki et al. 2019** (*Sports* 7(7):154, "Nutrition recommendations for bodybuilders in the off-season"):
  - Surplus of **~10–20% above maintenance**, targeting **~0.25–0.5% BW/week** for novice and intermediate lifters.
  - Advanced lifters should use a more conservative surplus; about **2 kg/month may be excessive** for them.
  - Adjust based on the observed rate and body-composition changes.
  - Faster gain immediately post-show may be reasonable for recovery.
  - Off-season research is sparse.
  - Source: [PMC6680710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/); [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/7/7/154); [PubMed 31247944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31247944/)
- **Helms et al. 2023** (*Sports Medicine – Open*): 21 trained lifters randomized (17 completed), 8 weeks, resistance training 3×/wk. Groups were maintenance, a **5% surplus**, and a **15% surplus**.
  - The 15% group gained more bench 1RM, but there was no group effect on squat 1RM or muscle thickness.
  - The 15% group gained more skinfold thickness than maintenance (BF10=4.2).
  - Across participants, body-mass change strongly predicted skinfold change (BF10=14.3, R²=0.49) but only weakly predicted biceps thickness.
  - Conclusion: faster gain mostly adds fat.
  - Sources: [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/de93156f-23a9-492a-8a85-438233a7e6bf/full); [preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-3184470/v1.pdf); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/ab6ce09af2cc4daa9a1c2e9b88e1b1e9); [BarBend coverage](https://barbend.com/layne-norton-build-muscle-without-caloric-surplus-study/)
- **Expected monthly lean gain by training age** (non-peer-reviewed aggregators; numbers vary):
  - Beginners: about **0.75–1.0 kg/month**; some sources say "2–4 lb"
  - Intermediates: about **0.4–0.6 kg/month**
  - Advanced: about **0.1–0.3 kg/month**
  - Sources: [Fitbod](https://fitbod.me/blog/muscle-gain-in-a-month/); [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/how-much-muscle-can-you-gain-in-a-month); [Peloton](https://onepeloton.com/blog/how-much-muscle-can-you-gain-in-a-month)
- An example for an intermediate lifter is about 0.3% BW/week (~0.5 lb/wk), often implemented as roughly +200–300 kcal over TDEE. Larger surpluses do not speed muscle growth but do increase fat — [Fitness Volt](https://fitnessvolt.com/weight-gain-bulking-guide/)
- RP (Renaissance Periodization) has guidance on choosing a muscle-gain phase (content not retrieved) — [RP help center](https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/34706604309783-How-to-choose-an-effective-muscle-gain-phase)

### Inferences
- **Suggested presets by training age:**

  | Training age | Weekly gain | Approx. lean gain | Approx. surplus |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Beginner (<1 yr) | 0.5% BW/wk | ~1–2 kg/month | +300–500 kcal |
  | Intermediate | 0.25–0.35%/wk | — | +200–300 kcal |
  | Advanced | ≤0.25%/wk (~0.5–1 kg/month) | — | +100–200 kcal |

  For women, use the lower end of each band in absolute kg, since their absolute muscle-gain capacity is lower. Relative (%) hypertrophy rates are broadly similar between sexes, but this is background knowledge not verified this session.
- **Initial jump:** in the first 1–2 weeks of a bulk, expect about 1–2 kg from glycogen, water and gut content when carbs and food volume rise. The app should exclude this from the surplus calculation or show a "settling" banner, rather than cutting calories in response.
- **Fat:muscle ratio:** a realistic expectation for trained lifters on a lean bulk is that a substantial share, often half or more, of weight gained is fat. Helms 2023's regression supports the idea that faster gain means more fat. A precise ratio was not found in a primary source this session.
- **When to stop a bulk / mini-cuts:** commonly cited heuristics are:
  - End or pause the bulk at about 15–20% body fat for men or about 25–30% for women.
  - Then run a mini-cut of 2–6 weeks at about 0.75–1% BW/wk.
  - These are coach heuristics (Helms *Pyramid*, RP); no primary source was found this session.

### Gaps
- Primary sources for body-fat stop thresholds and mini-cut protocols (Helms *Muscle & Strength Pyramid* and RP not retrievable).
- Women-specific bulking data (rates, menstrual considerations).
- Peer-reviewed estimates of muscle:fat partitioning by training status.

## Maintenance phases and recomposition

### Takeaway
Maintenance is a legitimate phase: after a cut (to consolidate), between bulks, or indefinitely for beginners and people returning to training. Helms 2023 suggests trained lifters at maintenance did not gain significantly less muscle thickness over 8 weeks than those in a 5% or 15% surplus. That supports "recomp at maintenance" as viable, especially for beginners, detrained lifters, and people with higher body fat.

### Cited Findings
- No group effect on muscle thickness between maintenance, 5% and 15% surpluses in trained lifters over 8 weeks — [Helms 2023, AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/de93156f-23a9-492a-8a85-438233a7e6bf/full); prior work suggested a surplus isn't strictly required for muscle gain — [BarBend](https://barbend.com/layne-norton-build-muscle-without-caloric-surplus-study/)
- Adaptation measured after 4 weeks of weight stability is small (−54 kcal/d), which supports using a stable maintenance period to "normalize" before reassessing — [Martins 2020, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32386226/)
- MATADOR's 2-week maintenance blocks (2 weeks restriction alternating with 2 weeks at energy balance) are a validated template for structured maintenance periods within a cut — [PMC5803575](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803575)

### Inferences
- **Maintenance target:** keep weekly trend change within ±0.1–0.2% BW/wk. Do not adjust targets on small drift.
- **Recomp mode:** eat at maintenance with high protein (about 1.6–2.2 g/kg) and progressive training. Show waist measurements, photos and strength alongside weight, since scale weight will barely move.

### Gaps
- Dedicated recomposition RCT data (e.g., Barakat et al. 2020 review) was not retrieved this session.

## Adaptive TDEE estimation and weekly check-in logic

### Takeaway
MacroFactor-style expenditure is a back-calculation: **TDEE ≈ average logged intake − (Δ trend weight × energy density)**. It uses trend (smoothed) weight, not raw scale weight. It assumes logging is complete and needs about 3–4 weeks of consistent data to confidently detect a real expenditure change. The fixed 7,700 kcal/kg (3,500 kcal/lb) constant overestimates the energy in early and lean-person weight change. Dynamic modelling (Hall) and smoothing compensate for this.

### Cited Findings
- **Core equation:** "Calories in – Change in stored energy = Calories out." Example: a trend implying a 200 kcal/day surplus at 3,000 kcal intake gives TDEE ≈ 2,800 kcal/day — [MacroFactor help: Expenditure](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/20-expenditure)
- Expenditure is calculated from **trend weight changes, not raw scale numbers**. Large past weight edits moved average expenditure only slightly — [MacroFactor help #209](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/209-should-i-still-log-my-weight-if-i-feel-like-i-m-bloated-constipated-or-retaining-water)
- Logging must be thorough. Systematic under-logging causes the algorithm to keep lowering targets until the goal rate is achieved, so the target self-corrects but the TDEE number is biased low — [MacroFactor help #140](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/140-do-i-need-to-log-everything-i-eat-and-drink-to-have-an-accurate-expenditure-and-use-macrofactor-s-coaching-features)
- **Adherence-neutral:** recommendations are based on actual intake and weight change, not on how well the user followed the targets — [MacroFactor help #222](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)
- After **3–4 weeks of consistent data** showing a slowed rate of loss, the algorithm becomes much more confident that expenditure has dropped. In other words, it is deliberately slow to react — [MacroFactor help #222](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/222-how-does-macrofactor-make-adjustments-for-a-weight-gain-or-weight-loss-goal)
- The Energy Balance widget uses a simple 1-month average of expenditure and intake. When it disagrees with the weight-trend widget, the trend-implied deficit or surplus is likely more accurate — [MacroFactor help #224](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/224-interpreting-the-energy-balance-widget)
- MacroFactor applies "expenditure modifiers" on top of the base calculation — [MacroFactor: expenditure modifiers](https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/)
- I found no independent validation of MacroFactor's algorithm accuracy (third-party reviews mostly restate MacroFactor's description) — [calorie-trackers.com review](https://calorie-trackers.com/reviews/macrofactor/)
- **7,700 kcal/kg caveats (Hall):**
  - The biggest error in the 3,500-kcal rule is ignoring dynamic changes in energy balance. A corrected constant alone does not fix it.
  - The energy density of lost weight is significantly lower than the rule when initial body fat is below about 25 kg. The rule roughly fits only when body fat is above about 30 kg.
  - The fat:lean composition of loss changes over time.
  - Early weight loss is faster than the rule predicts.
  - Energy density of fat-free mass is reported at 1.1–1.8 kcal/g.
  - Sources: [Hall & Chow, PMC3810417](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3810417/); [Hall 2008, PMC2376744](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2376744); [Thomas et al., PMC3859816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3859816/); [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/16D465BDCCCFA52B617B3CC35F1BBADE)

### Inferences (implementable algorithm sketch)
1. **Trend weight:** use an exponentially weighted moving average of daily weights, α ≈ 0.1 (roughly a 10–20-day effective window). Hacker's Diet uses 0.1, and MacroFactor's exact α is not published. Interpolate missing days and cap single-day outliers (e.g., more than 3% from trend).
2. **TDEE estimate:** over a rolling 14–28-day window, TDEE = mean(logged intake) − (slope of trend in kg/day × ρ).
   - Use ρ ≈ 7,700 kcal/kg for steady-state loss in people with higher body fat.
   - Use a lower ρ (e.g., ~5,000–6,000) in the first 2–3 weeks of a phase and for lean users, since early weight change is heavy in glycogen and water.
   - For gains, use about 5,500–7,000 kcal/kg, because the mix of lean tissue and glycogen lowers the effective density.
   - These ρ values are inferences from Hall's work, not published constants.
3. **Smoothing and priors:** blend the TDEE estimate with the previous estimate (e.g., new = 0.8·old + 0.2·measured, weekly), starting from a Mifflin–St Jeor × activity prior. Limit week-to-week TDEE changes to about ±100–150 kcal.
4. **Weekly check-in:** target intake = TDEE + (goal rate kg/wk × ρ / 7).
   - Only change targets if the target shift is ≥50 kcal.
   - Freeze adjustments during the first 1–2 weeks after a phase change (water/glycogen settling) and for days the user flags as incomplete logging.
   - Do not "punish" non-adherence: adjustments are adherence-neutral, per MacroFactor.
5. **Phase-specific guardrails:**
   - Cut: floor of the higher of about 1,200 kcal (women) / 1,500 kcal (men) and BMR × 1.1.
   - Bulk: cap the surplus at +20% of TDEE (Iraki).
   - Reverse: never set intake above TDEE + 10%.
   - These floors are common practice, not sourced this session.

### Gaps
- MacroFactor's exact smoothing constants, priors, and modifier formulas are not public, and the blog and help pages were blocked here.
- No peer-reviewed validation of consumer adaptive-TDEE algorithms was found.

## Explaining phases to beginners and managing expectations

### Takeaway
Each phase should come with a short "what to expect" script that covers the expected scale behaviour, a realistic weekly rate, and when the app will adjust. The script should emphasise trend over daily weight, and that early water shifts are not fat or muscle.

### Cited Findings
- Daily weights are noisy, so trend weight should drive decisions. Users should keep logging when bloated or constipated — [MacroFactor help #209](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/209-should-i-still-log-my-weight-if-i-feel-like-i-m-bloated-constipated-or-retaining-water)
- Early weight loss outpaces the 3,500/7,700 rule, then slows — [PMC3859816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3859816/)
- Metabolic slowdown is real but modest (about −50 kcal/d at stability). It lengthens a diet but doesn't prevent weight maintenance — [Martins 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7458773/); [SBS via MetaFilter](https://metafilter.com/196554/Reverse-Dieting)
- Diet breaks reduce hunger and irritability and improve alertness — [ICECAP secondary, PMC7906362](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7906362)
- Faster bulking mostly adds fat — [Helms 2023](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/de93156f-23a9-492a-8a85-438233a7e6bf/full)

### Inferences (suggested in-app copy, beginner level)
- **Cut:** "Expect a quick drop in week 1 (mostly water), then about 0.5–1% of your weight per week. Some weeks will show nothing, then a drop. Your hunger and energy may dip as the weeks go on; that's normal, and a 1–2 week maintenance break can help. We adjust weekly using your trend, not today's weigh-in."
- **Bulk:** "Expect a 1–2 kg jump in the first couple of weeks from stored carbs and water. That isn't fat. After that, aim for slow gain: about 0.25–0.5% per week. Gaining faster mostly adds fat, not muscle."
- **Maintenance / after a cut:** "Moving to maintenance will bump the scale up 1–2 kg from water and glycogen. Your metabolism isn't broken; it's a small effect. You can step up gradually if big jumps in food feel uncomfortable, but research doesn't show slow increases prevent regain."
- **Recomp:** "Weight may stay flat while your waist shrinks and lifts go up. Track measurements and photos."
